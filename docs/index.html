African money shouldn't travel via London to get from Lagos to Johannesburg. Now it doesn't have to.

Open Corridor is a payment network between licensed banks. Customers are credited in seconds, every promise is anchored on a public ledger neither bank controls, and banks settle only the netted difference — per corridor, on the rail the two banks choose.

How a payment works

One payment's journey, in five steps.

1 · Send

The order

Someone asks their bank to pay a person at another bank, in another country: who is paying, who receives, how much.

2 · Promise

The public commitment

The sending bank debits its customer and publishes a promise on a public ledger. The promise is a fingerprint of the payment — no names or amounts are visible to outsiders.

3 · Credit

Seconds, not days

The receiving bank checks the details against the published promise and pays its customer straight away — before any money moves between the banks.

4 · Position

The running score

Each bank keeps a running total of what it has promised but not yet paid the other — the score since they last settled up.

5 · Settle

Only the difference moves

At any time, either bank settles. Payments in both directions cancel out; only the difference is transferred, in one payment, at a locked rate.

Joining Open Corridor

Any bank can join using its existing systems and a small tech team. The connection point is the OBP Bank Node — an open-source gateway each bank runs on its own infrastructure, under its own control.

  • Open code, open logs — everything the node does can be inspected and audited.
  • Outbound only — the node dials out; nothing from outside connects into the bank's network.
  • No core rebuild — the node connects to the systems a bank already runs: two touchpoints, both inside the bank's network.
  • No resident engineers — banks operate the node themselves; integration help is available when wanted.
  • Verified participants — banks are admitted against the regulator's published list of licensed institutions.

Settlement

Open Corridor handles messaging, promises, and netting. Moving the settled value needs a rail — and the network takes no bet on a winner: the rail is a per-corridor choice between the two banks, not a design commitment.

Today — on-chain

Settle over Cardano. Exposure is minimal by construction: only the netted difference moves, the exchange rate is locked at settlement time, and the asset is held only briefly. Stablecoin settlement follows the same path — including regulated, fiat-backed assets and African stablecoins as they mature.

In parallel — bank rails, made efficient

Existing correspondent arrangements carry one netted settlement instead of every payment: fewer fees, one FX conversion at a locked rate — and customer speed no longer depends on the rail, because customers are credited at promise time.

Security

Built on the Open Bank Project stack, following the security standards European regulators already require of open banking.

  • Encrypted connections — HTTPS everywhere; system-to-system links use mutual TLS.
  • Verified participants — qualified digital certificates (eIDAS-style QWAC/QSEAL) under the corridor's trust framework.
  • Signed messages — digital signatures (JWS) make tampering detectable and every message attributable.
  • Strong auth — machine-to-machine OAuth 2.0; FAPI, the strictest OAuth profile for financial services, is supported.
Every stage of a payment — promise, price, settlement — leaves a tamper-proof, independently verifiable record. A regulator can confirm the whole trail without trusting any single bank's private database.
