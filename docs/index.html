Money shouldn't cross continents to reach a neighbour. Now it doesn't have to.

Open Corridor is a payment network between licensed banks. Customers are credited in seconds, every promise is anchored on a public ledger neither bank controls, and banks settle only the netted difference — per corridor, on the rail the two banks choose.

What Open Corridor changes

Five things banks get that today's cross-border payments don't give them.

  • Speed — the customer is credited when the promise arrives, in seconds, not when the money moves.
  • Netting — banks settle one net amount from time to time, instead of every payment.
  • Less money tied up — banks don't need money sitting in accounts at every correspondent in advance.
  • Shared evidence — each promise is a commitment on a public ledger that both banks can check, instead of each bank trusting the other's records.
  • Open and neutral — open-source software that any operator can run, rather than one network every bank must join.

How a payment works

One payment's journey, in five steps.

1 · Send

The order

Someone asks their bank to pay a person at another bank, in another country: who is paying, who receives, how much.

2 · Promise

The public commitment

The sending bank debits its customer and publishes a promise on a public ledger. The promise is a fingerprint of the payment — no names or amounts are visible to outsiders.

3 · Credit

Seconds, not days

The receiving bank checks the details against the published promise and pays its customer straight away — before any money moves between the banks.

4 · Position

The running score

Each bank keeps a running total of what it has promised but not yet paid the other — the score since they last settled up.

5 · Settle

Only the difference moves

At any time, either bank settles. Payments in both directions cancel out; only the difference is transferred, in one payment, at a locked rate.

The network

Open Corridor grows the way correspondent banking did: through banks with relationships in more than one place, not through treaties between networks.

Hosts

An operator runs an Open Corridor host for the banks in its region, under rules that fit their regulator. Joining a host is easy for a bank: same rules, same supervisor, faster payments with banks it already deals with. Two hosts never need an agreement with each other.

Hubs

A bank that is a member of two hosts links them. It carries payments between them, earning fees and the FX spread, and takes the risk between the two halves of a payment. Only the hub needs agreements on both sides. Banking groups and existing correspondents are natural hubs, and more hubs on the same link means more competition.

Visible reach

A host lists only its own members. A hub states on its own record which banks on other hosts it can reach, and keeps that statement fresh. A hub that stops confirming its reach drops out by itself. Anyone can see who reaches whom: regulators, sending banks and competing hubs.

A payment through a hub

1 · Send

Name the real beneficiary

The sending bank names the beneficiary's own bank. Its host picks a hub that can reach it, from the hubs the sending bank has approved.

2 · Leg 1

A conditional promise

The sending bank promises the hub, on condition that the beneficiary bank accepts the next leg before a deadline.

3 · Leg 2

The hub promises

The hub screens the payment and promises the beneficiary bank on the other host, with a shorter deadline. The beneficiary is credited in seconds.

4 · Accept

Proof on the ledger

The beneficiary bank posts its acceptance on the public ledger. The two hosts never talk to each other, but they read the same ledger, and that acceptance makes leg 1 final.

5 · Settle

Each pair settles

The sending bank and the hub settle under one host's rules; the hub and the beneficiary bank under the other's. If anything fails along the way, the deadline passes and nothing is final: a delay, not lost money.

The sending bank stays in control of its route. It approves the hubs it will use and the countries a payment may never pass through. Its host picks a route for each payment within those limits, and records which hub was used and why. The sending bank, the hub and the beneficiary bank each screen the payment, as in correspondent banking.

Joining Open Corridor

There are two ways in: run a host for the banks in your region, or join a host as a bank.

Operators: run a host

An operator runs an Open Corridor host on the open-source Open Bank Project platform, for the banks in its region.

  • Your rules — set the rulebook to fit your regulator, and decide which banks join and at what pace.
  • Verified members — admit banks against the regulator's published list of licensed institutions.
  • Your settlement rails — the banks on your host settle on the rails your rules allow.
  • Reach beyond your region — banks that are members of your host and another link the two. You never need an agreement with another operator.
  • Open code — you, your members and your regulator can inspect exactly what runs.

Banks: join a host

A bank joins a host using its existing systems and a small tech team. The connection point is the OBP Bank Node — an open-source gateway each bank runs on its own infrastructure, under its own control.

  • Open code, open logs — everything the node does can be inspected and audited.
  • Outbound only — the node dials out; nothing from outside connects into the bank's network.
  • No core rebuild — the node connects to the systems a bank already runs: two touchpoints, both inside the bank's network.
  • No resident engineers — banks operate the node themselves; integration help is available when wanted.
  • One node, several hosts — a bank licensed in more than one region can join a host in each with the same node, and carry payments between them as a hub.

Settlement

Open Corridor handles messaging, promises, and netting. Moving the settled value needs a rail — and the network takes no bet on a winner: the rail is a per-corridor choice between the two banks, not a design commitment.

Today — on-chain

Settle over Cardano. Exposure is minimal by construction: only the netted difference moves, the exchange rate is locked at settlement time, and the asset is held only briefly. Stablecoin settlement follows the same path — including regulated, fiat-backed assets and local-currency stablecoins as they mature.

In parallel — bank rails, made efficient

Existing correspondent arrangements carry one netted settlement instead of every payment: fewer fees, one FX conversion at a locked rate — and customer speed no longer depends on the rail, because customers are credited at promise time.

Security

Built on the Open Bank Project stack, following the security standards European regulators already require of open banking.

  • Encrypted connections — HTTPS everywhere; system-to-system links use mutual TLS.
  • Verified participants — qualified digital certificates (eIDAS-style QWAC/QSEAL) under the corridor's trust framework.
  • Signed messages — digital signatures (JWS) make tampering detectable and every message attributable.
  • Strong auth — machine-to-machine OAuth 2.0; FAPI, the strictest OAuth profile for financial services, is supported.
Every stage of a payment — promise, price, settlement — leaves a tamper-proof, independently verifiable record. A regulator can confirm the whole trail without trusting any single bank's private database.
